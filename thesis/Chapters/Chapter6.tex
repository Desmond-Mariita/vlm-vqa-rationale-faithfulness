This chapter reports the evidence for RQ1 and RQ2. All Stage~1
cells use the same 2,653 records and 2,400 source frames. The primary Stage~2
set contains 63,672 generations from one RTX~5090 environment.
Intervals are 95\% percentile intervals from 10,000 bootstrap replicates
clustered by source frame with seed~42.

\section{RQ1: Stage~1 answer behaviour}
\label{sec:results-rq1}

\subsection{Accuracy across the six image conditions}
\label{sec:results-rq1-accuracy}

Accuracy with the source image ranged from 0.805 to 0.815 across arms. The
interventions separated the
conditions much more clearly (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-battery}). Mismatch produced
the largest accuracy loss in every arm, with gaps from 0.296 to 0.374 relative
to source. Grey was next, with gaps from 0.189 to 0.322. Mask and noise had
intermediate gaps, while mirror remained close to source.

\input{Tables/final/rq1_battery}

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1-accuracy} shows the same pattern without the interval
inventory, and Table~\ref{tab:rq1-gaps} gives every gap relative to source with
its interval. The two description arms retained more accuracy under grey,
mismatch, mask, and noise than their matched arms without descriptions. Polygon
overlays alone did not produce a uniform advantage: condition differences
between \texttt{point} and \texttt{plain} changed direction.

After every image pixel was replaced by uniform grey, accuracy remained between
0.483 and 0.626 in every arm, above the 0.25 chance level for four choices. Every arm still received the question and
answer choices; the two description arms also retained their descriptions, and
learned regularities could contribute in all arms. Source accuracy alone therefore
cannot establish dependence on the image.

Mismatch produced the lowest accuracy in every arm: 0.438 for
\texttt{plain}, 0.435 for \texttt{point}, 0.487 for
\texttt{plain\_desc}, and 0.519 for \texttt{point\_desc}.

\subsection{Sufficiency Gap and description comparisons}
\label{sec:results-rq1-sufficiency}

Sufficiency Gap is source accuracy minus grey accuracy. It was 0.322 for
\texttt{plain}, 0.319 for \texttt{point}, 0.221 for
\texttt{plain\_desc}, and 0.189 for \texttt{point\_desc}. The
comparisons between arms with and without descriptions were negative in both matched arm
pairs (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-sufficiency}). Thus description arms lost less answer
accuracy when the image was removed.

\subsection{Correctness transitions}
\label{sec:results-rq1-transitions}

The transition decomposition shows how the accuracy gaps arose
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1-transitions-compact}). Under grey, the
C$\rightarrow$W rate was 46.09\% for \texttt{plain} and 33.33\% for
\texttt{plain\_desc}. It was 44.45\% for \texttt{point} and 27.88\% for
\texttt{point\_desc}. Each rate uses the records that were correct under source
in that arm. As planned comparisons, those differences were $-0.1276$
[$-0.1468$, $-0.1085$] for \texttt{plain\_desc} minus \texttt{plain} and
$-0.1657$ [$-0.1876$, $-0.1442$] for \texttt{point\_desc} minus
\texttt{point}. The corresponding W$\rightarrow$C counts changed little:
131 versus 133, and 108 versus 102. Grey accuracy was below source accuracy in
every arm because C$\rightarrow$W counts exceeded W$\rightarrow$C counts.
The lower C$\rightarrow$W rates in the description arms account for most of
their smaller source-to-grey accuracy gaps. Because each arm has its own
checkpoint, these differences describe the adapted arms.

Mismatch produced the largest C$\rightarrow$W count and churn in every arm
(\mbox{Table}~\ref{tab:rq1-churn-compact}). Mirror provides the counterexample to
relying only on net accuracy. Its net gaps were 0.49--1.13 percentage points,
yet 5.24--7.50\% of records changed correctness state. Correct and wrong
movements partly cancelled in the aggregate. Appendix~\ref{sec:app-rq1-transitions}
gives the complete table of all twenty arm-condition correctness transitions.

The qualitative audit examined 16 mechanically selected W$\rightarrow$C cases.
It found answer-choice artefacts, scene or choice
ambiguity, misleading or irrelevant mismatch content, apparent decision
instability, one case in which the masked image still showed visible support for
the keyed answer, and two cases it could not resolve. One record contained four
nearly identical choices that expressed the same answer, so an index change
appeared as W$\rightarrow$C without a meaningful content change. The counts are
too small to read as frequencies. The audit supports treating W$\rightarrow$C as
an output transition. The full
sample is reported in Appendix~\ref{sec:app-w2c}.

\subsection{Direct answer to RQ1}
\label{sec:results-rq1-answer}

\begin{samepage}
Stage~1 answer behaviour changed with the available visual evidence. Mismatch
was most disruptive, grey was next, mask and noise were intermediate, and mirror
produced little net accuracy loss. The description arms lost less accuracy under
grey, mismatch, mask, and noise, mainly because fewer source-correct predictions
became wrong. Polygon overlays did not show a consistent advantage across the
image conditions.
\end{samepage}

\section{RQ2: Stage~2 rationale behaviour}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

\subsection{Parser coverage}
\label{sec:results-rq2-coverage}

The primary set contained 63,672 raw generations. The tolerant parser
recovered 63,664 non-empty final rationale spans (99.987\%). Of 53,060 possible
pairs relative to source, 53,049 were usable (99.979\%); every cell retained at
least 2,651 pairs and 2,398 source frames. Thirteen outputs ended at the length
limit, and no generation or record error was recorded.

Strict and tolerant parsing differed most in \texttt{point\_desc}. Strict
final tags were present in 2,435 of 2,653 mismatch generations and 2,603 of
2,653 source generations, while the tolerant parser recovered all final spans in
both cells. These differences are formatting limitations and remain visible in
Appendix~\ref{sec:app-rq2-coverage}.

\subsection{Primary BERTScore Drift results}
\label{sec:results-rq2-drift}

Rationale text responded to every visual intervention
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2-drift} and Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-drift}). The common broad
ordering was mirror, then mask and noise, then grey, then mismatch from lower to
higher mean Drift. Mismatch
produced the highest mean in every arm, from 0.0788 to 0.0890. Mirror produced
the lowest, from 0.0496 to 0.0604.

Under grey, \texttt{plain\_desc} minus \texttt{plain} Drift was
$-0.0090$ [$-0.0107$, $-0.0073$]. The \texttt{point\_desc} minus

Figure~\ref{fig:rq2-paired-example} shows what a single paired comparison looks

rationale introduces scene detail that the uniform input cannot support: it
places them behind a tree. Under mismatch the rationale appears to follow the
unrelated replacement scene, describing people approaching a seated pair. That
text may fit parts of the wrong image, but it cannot support the source record's
question and answer. Raw Drift was
0.0822 under grey and 0.0884 under mismatch, against arm means of 0.0835 and

One record cannot show a mean. It does show what the measure is comparing.

\subsection{Wrong-answer control and empirical scale references}
\label{sec:results-rq2-anchors}

The wrong-answer control compared image removal from source to grey with a
change to the supplied answer on the same 200 records per arm.
Drift from the wrong answer was greater in all four arms
(Table~\ref{tab:rq2-known-change}). Every paired difference interval was above
zero. The direction also survived the reasoning span and final rationales with
answer choices masked.

\subsection{Direct answer to RQ2}
\label{sec:results-rq2-answer}

\begin{samepage}
With the supplied gold answer fixed, Stage~2 rationale text changed under every
visual intervention. Mean Drift was greatest under mismatch, next under grey,
intermediate under mask and noise, and lowest under mirror. The description arms
had lower source-to-grey Drift than their matched arms without descriptions. In the
200-record wrong-answer control, changing the supplied answer produced greater Drift
than matched grey replacement in every arm.
\end{samepage}

\subsection{Secondary predicted-answer evidence}
\label{sec:results-pred-a}

Table~\ref{tab:pred-a-compact} reports the PRED-A regime, which uses source
images only and in which Stage~2 received an answer selected by Stage~1 instead
of the gold answer (Section~\ref{sec:setup-pred-a}). Usable final-span coverage
was 91.86\% for \texttt{plain}, 39.84\% for \texttt{point}, 61.21\% for
\texttt{plain\_desc}, and 97.02\% for \texttt{point\_desc}. Because coverage
varied this widely, a rate calculated only over successful generations does not
describe the full regime.

Among usable outputs, conditional CMC ranged from 0.5369 to 0.6305, so the judge
recovered the supplied answer from a little over half of the rationales in every
arm. Once coverage is included, pipeline CMC ranged from 0.2341 to 0.6118. The
two rates order the arms differently: \texttt{point} has the second-highest
conditional value and the lowest pipeline value. Moderate recoverability among
successful generations therefore does not imply that the regime performs well
end to end. CMC records coherence with the supplied answer
(Section~\ref{sec:framework-pred-a}).
